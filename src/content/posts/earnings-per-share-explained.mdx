**Earnings per share**, or EPS, measures the profit available to common shareholders for each weighted-average share. It connects a company’s total earnings with an individual unit of ownership.

EPS is widely used in valuation, but it is an accounting measure—not the same as cash generated per share.

## Basic EPS formula

> Basic EPS = (net income − preferred dividends) ÷ weighted-average common shares

Suppose a company reports:

- Net income of $120 million
- Preferred dividends of $20 million
- Weighted-average common shares of 50 million

Profit available to common shareholders is $100 million:

> ($120 million − $20 million) ÷ 50 million = $2 basic EPS

Preferred dividends are subtracted because that portion of earnings is not available to common shareholders.

## Why use weighted-average shares?

Share count can change during the year through issuance, repurchases, acquisitions, or employee compensation. Using the ending count would wrongly treat every share as if it existed for the full period.

If a company had 100 million shares for half the year and 120 million for the other half, its simplified weighted average would be 110 million.

Stock splits are generally reflected retroactively so per-share figures remain comparable across periods.

## Basic EPS versus diluted EPS

Basic EPS includes shares currently outstanding. **Diluted EPS** also considers securities that could become common shares, such as:

- Employee stock options
- Restricted stock units
- Convertible debt
- Convertible preferred stock
- Warrants

If potential conversion would reduce EPS, those securities are dilutive and included under accounting rules. If conversion would increase EPS or reduce a loss per share, they are generally excluded as anti-dilutive.

Investors usually focus on diluted EPS because it gives a more conservative picture of each share’s claim.

## A dilution example

Assume the company earns $100 million for common shareholders:

- 50 million basic shares produce $2.00 basic EPS.
- 55 million diluted shares produce about $1.82 diluted EPS.

The business earned the same total profit, but potential additional shares reduce the amount attributable to each share.

Track diluted share count over time. A company may announce buybacks while employee compensation causes total diluted shares to stay flat or rise.

## Trailing and forward EPS

**Trailing EPS** uses reported earnings, commonly from the last four quarters.

**Forward EPS** is an analyst or investor estimate for a future period. It is useful for valuation but uncertain.

The [P/E ratio](/blog/pe-ratio-explained) must be labeled consistently. Dividing price by trailing EPS creates trailing P/E; using estimated EPS creates forward P/E.

## GAAP EPS versus adjusted EPS

Companies often present adjusted or non-GAAP EPS that excludes selected expenses. Adjustments may help isolate unusual events, but management decides what to exclude.

Common exclusions include:

- Restructuring charges
- Acquisition costs
- Stock-based compensation
- Asset impairments
- Gains or losses on investments

An expense is not irrelevant merely because it is non-cash or described as one-time. If a company excludes similar costs every year, treat them as part of normal economics.

Reconcile adjusted EPS with the standardized figure in audited [financial statements](/blog/how-to-read-financial-statements).

## How can EPS grow?

EPS can increase because:

1. Revenue grows.
2. Profit margins improve.
3. Interest or tax expense declines.
4. The company repurchases shares.
5. A one-time gain increases net income.

Not all EPS growth has equal quality. Growth driven by stronger recurring operations is generally more durable than growth produced only by financial engineering or temporary tax changes.

## EPS and stock buybacks

If net income remains $100 million and shares decline from 50 million to 40 million, EPS rises from $2.00 to $2.50 even though total profit did not grow.

That can benefit remaining owners if shares were repurchased below intrinsic value and the balance sheet remains healthy. Buying overvalued shares or borrowing excessively can destroy value despite higher EPS.

Read our guide to [stock buybacks](/blog/how-stock-buybacks-work) for a fuller explanation.

## Limitations of EPS

EPS can mislead when:

- Earnings include large one-time items.
- The business is highly cyclical.
- Accounting profit differs persistently from cash flow.
- Debt-funded buybacks increase financial risk.
- Stock compensation causes dilution.
- Comparisons ignore different accounting policies.
- Negative EPS makes P/E meaningless.

EPS also does not account for capital required to produce earnings. Two companies with equal EPS can have very different debt, share counts, cash needs, and values.

## A practical EPS review

Compare:

- Basic and diluted EPS
- GAAP and adjusted EPS
- EPS growth and net-income growth
- Net income and operating cash flow
- Diluted share count over several years
- Management adjustments across reporting periods

Then determine which business driver changed and whether that change appears repeatable.

## The bottom line

EPS allocates common shareholder profit across the weighted-average share count. Diluted EPS is generally the more cautious measure because it includes potential shares. Use EPS as one input, verify its quality against cash flow and dilution, and never value a company on a per-share number without understanding how it was produced.
