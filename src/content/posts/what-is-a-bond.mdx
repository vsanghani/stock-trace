A **bond** is a debt security. When you buy one, you are lending money to a government, company, or other issuer. In return, the issuer promises to make specified payments and repay principal according to the bond’s terms.

Stocks represent ownership; bonds represent a lending relationship. Bondholders generally rank ahead of common shareholders if an issuer fails, but they can still lose money.

## The basic parts of a bond

- **Face value:** The amount repaid at maturity, commonly $1,000 for a corporate bond.
- **Coupon rate:** The stated annual interest rate applied to face value.
- **Coupon payment:** The cash interest paid periodically.
- **Maturity date:** When principal is scheduled to be repaid.
- **Market price:** What investors currently pay for the bond.
- **Yield:** Return implied by the bond’s price and cash flows.

A $1,000 bond with a 5% coupon normally pays $50 of interest per year. If payments are semiannual, it would pay $25 every six months.

## Bond price and yield move in opposite directions

Suppose new bonds with similar risk begin offering 6%. An existing 5% bond becomes less attractive, so its market price generally falls until its yield is competitive.

If market rates fall to 4%, the existing 5% coupon becomes more attractive and its price generally rises.

This inverse relationship is fundamental:

> Market yields rise → existing bond prices generally fall

> Market yields fall → existing bond prices generally rise

An investor holding a default-free bond until maturity may still receive its promised face value, but selling before maturity can produce a gain or loss.

## Coupon rate, current yield, and yield to maturity

These measures answer different questions.

**Coupon rate** is fixed relative to face value:

> Annual coupon ÷ face value

**Current yield** compares annual coupon income with the current market price:

> Annual coupon ÷ market price

If a $1,000-face-value bond paying $50 annually trades for $900, current yield is approximately 5.56%.

**Yield to maturity**, or YTM, estimates the annualized return if the bond is held to maturity, all scheduled payments are made, and coupons can be reinvested at the calculated rate. It accounts for coupon income and the difference between purchase price and face value.

YTM is a model, not a guaranteed return. Default, calls, reinvestment rates, taxes, and transaction costs can change the outcome.

## Major types of bonds

- **Government bonds:** Issued by national governments.
- **Municipal bonds:** Issued by states, cities, or public entities.
- **Corporate bonds:** Issued by companies.
- **Agency bonds:** Issued or guaranteed by government-related organizations.
- **Mortgage-backed and asset-backed securities:** Supported by pools of loans or receivables.

Tax treatment, guarantees, liquidity, and default risk vary by security and jurisdiction.

## Credit risk

Credit risk is the possibility that an issuer will miss payments or fail to repay principal. Credit-rating agencies assign opinions ranging from higher-quality investment grade to lower-quality high yield.

Ratings can be wrong or change after market prices have already moved. Investors should also examine:

- Debt relative to cash flow
- Interest coverage
- Cash reserves and liquidity
- Debt maturity schedule
- Collateral and seniority
- Industry stability
- Contract terms, or covenants

Higher yields often compensate for greater risk; they are not free additional return.

## Interest-rate risk and duration

**Duration** estimates how sensitive a bond’s price is to changes in yield. As a rough approximation, a duration of seven means the price may fall about 7% if yields rise by one percentage point, or rise about 7% if yields fall by one point.

Actual results differ because duration changes and the price-yield relationship is curved. Longer maturities and lower coupons generally create greater rate sensitivity.

## Other bond risks

- **Inflation risk:** Fixed payments lose purchasing power when inflation rises.
- **Reinvestment risk:** Coupons may need to be reinvested at lower rates.
- **Call risk:** An issuer may repay a callable bond early, often when rates fall.
- **Liquidity risk:** A bond may be difficult to sell near an estimated fair price.
- **Currency risk:** Foreign-bond returns change with exchange rates.

## Individual bonds versus bond ETFs

An individual bond has a stated maturity, assuming the issuer performs. A bond [ETF](/blog/what-is-an-etf) continually buys and sells holdings and generally has no single maturity date.

Bond ETFs provide diversification and easier trading, but their share prices fluctuate and do not automatically return to a fixed value on a particular date. Compare duration, credit quality, yield, fees, and holdings.

## Why investors own bonds

Bonds can provide income, preserve capital for a defined period, or diversify equity exposure. They can still decline at the same time as stocks, particularly when inflation or rates rise sharply.

The right allocation depends on goals, time horizon, liquidity needs, taxes, and tolerance for price movement. Read our guide to [portfolio diversification](/blog/diversification-and-portfolio-risk) for the broader context.

## The bottom line

A bond is a tradable loan with contractual cash flows. Its price reflects prevailing interest rates, credit risk, time to maturity, and contract terms. Look beyond the coupon: yield, duration, seniority, and the issuer’s ability to pay determine the risk you are accepting.
