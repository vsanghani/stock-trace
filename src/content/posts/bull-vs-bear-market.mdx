A **bull market** is an extended period of generally rising asset prices. A **bear market** is an extended period of generally falling prices. The terms describe market direction and investor behavior; they do not predict what comes next.

Financial media commonly labels a decline of at least 20% from a recent high as a bear market. A 20% rise from a low is often called a bull market. These are conventions, not laws, and different indexes can be in different cycles at the same time.

## What happens in a bull market?

Bull markets often feature:

- Rising prices across a broad group of securities
- Improving earnings expectations
- Greater willingness to accept risk
- Easier access to capital
- Stronger investor confidence
- More initial public offerings and acquisition activity

Bull markets do not move upward every day. Corrections, sector declines, and recessions in individual industries can occur while a broad index remains in an uptrend.

As prices rise, valuations can become more demanding. Strong recent returns may encourage investors to underestimate risk or chase assets they do not understand.

## What happens in a bear market?

Bear markets often include:

- Falling stock prices and valuation multiples
- Weakening economic or earnings expectations
- Higher volatility
- Tighter financial conditions
- Reduced appetite for speculative assets
- Investor focus shifting from growth to survival and liquidity

Not every bear market accompanies a recession, and not every recession produces the same market decline. Markets anticipate future conditions, so prices may fall before economic data weakens and recover before the economy visibly improves.

## Bear market, correction, and crash

These labels are related but not identical:

- A **pullback** is a modest decline from a recent peak.
- A **correction** is commonly described as a decline of at least 10% but less than 20%.
- A **bear market** commonly refers to a decline of 20% or more.
- A **crash** describes an unusually rapid, severe decline and has no universal percentage definition.

Labels are assigned after thresholds are crossed. They do not tell investors how deep a decline will become or how quickly it will reverse.

## What drives market cycles?

Market prices reflect expected future cash flows and the returns investors demand. Important drivers include:

- Economic growth and recession risk
- Corporate earnings and margins
- Interest rates and inflation
- Credit availability
- Fiscal and monetary policy
- Geopolitical shocks
- Starting valuation
- Investor positioning and sentiment

The same news can have different effects depending on expectations. Weak economic data might push stocks lower because profits are threatened, or higher because investors expect interest-rate cuts.

## How long do bull and bear markets last?

There is no fixed duration. Historically, broad equity bull markets have often lasted longer than bear markets, while bear-market declines have often been sharper. Historical averages conceal a wide range and do not provide a countdown clock.

Market timing requires two successful decisions: when to exit and when to return. Missing a small number of strong recovery days can materially reduce long-term returns, and some of the best and worst days occur close together.

## What can long-term investors do?

### Revisit goals and time horizon

Money needed soon should not depend on a volatile asset recovering on schedule. Maintain an allocation consistent with when funds will be required.

### Review financial resilience

During difficult markets, companies with recurring cash flow and manageable debt generally have more options than those dependent on refinancing. Learn to assess this in [financial statements](/blog/how-to-read-financial-statements).

### Rebalance deliberately

A decline can push stocks below a target allocation. Rebalancing may call for buying, but only when the original allocation still fits the investor’s capacity and goals.

### Avoid leverage you cannot sustain

Borrowing can force sales when prices are depressed. [Options](/blog/what-are-stock-options) and margin can create losses much larger or faster than unleveraged ownership.

### Separate price changes from thesis changes

A lower price can improve prospective return if business fundamentals remain intact. It can also reflect permanent deterioration. Reassess revenue, competitive position, balance-sheet risk, and valuation instead of reacting to the chart alone.

## Common behavioral mistakes

- Buying solely because prices have recently risen
- Selling solely because prices have recently fallen
- Abandoning a long-term allocation after losses
- Assuming every decline will recover quickly
- Concentrating in the previous cycle’s winners
- Treating emergency savings as investment capital
- Following confident forecasts without examining evidence

A written plan can reduce decisions driven by fear or excitement.

## Can investors profit in both environments?

Different assets and strategies respond differently across cycles, but no approach works reliably in every bear or bull market. Short selling, put options, and inverse funds can benefit from declines but introduce timing, cost, leverage, and potentially substantial loss.

For most investors, controlling allocation, [diversifying exposures](/blog/diversification-and-portfolio-risk), maintaining liquidity, and avoiding forced selling are more repeatable than predicting market turning points.

## The bottom line

Bull and bear markets describe broad periods of rising and falling prices. They are normal parts of investing, not actionable forecasts by themselves. Build a portfolio for multiple conditions, maintain enough liquidity for near-term needs, and base decisions on goals and business facts rather than market labels.
