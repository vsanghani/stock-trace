There is no formula that reveals a stock’s objectively correct price. Valuation is the process of estimating what a business is worth based on the cash it may generate, the prices investors pay for comparable businesses, or the income it distributes.

The result should be a **range**, not a single magic number. Small changes to growth, margins, or discount rates can materially change an estimate.

## First, separate share price from company value

Two figures prevent common mistakes:

> Equity value, or market capitalization = share price × diluted shares outstanding

> Share price = estimated equity value ÷ diluted shares outstanding

If a company is worth an estimated $10 billion and has 200 million diluted shares, its estimated value is $50 per share.

Use diluted shares because options, restricted stock, and convertible securities may increase the number of claims on the business. Also remember that a stock split changes the share count and share price but does not, by itself, change total company value.

## Method 1: Discounted cash flow

A discounted cash flow, or DCF, model values a company by estimating the present value of future cash it can produce.

### Step 1: Forecast free cash flow

Free cash flow is commonly approximated as:

> Cash from operations − capital expenditures

Assume a company produced $100 million in free cash flow last year. You estimate it will grow 8% annually for five years:

- Year 1: $108.0 million
- Year 2: $116.6 million
- Year 3: $126.0 million
- Year 4: $136.0 million
- Year 5: $146.9 million

### Step 2: Choose a discount rate

Future dollars are worth less than dollars today, and uncertain cash flows deserve a risk adjustment. The discount rate represents both ideas. A higher rate produces a lower valuation.

For illustration, assume a 10% discount rate. Each annual cash flow is divided by 1.10 raised to the number of years in the future.

### Step 3: Estimate terminal value

Because a business may continue beyond the explicit forecast, analysts estimate a terminal value. One common formula is:

> Terminal value = final-year cash flow × (1 + perpetual growth rate) ÷ (discount rate − perpetual growth rate)

Using a 3% perpetual growth rate, the example terminal value at the end of year five is approximately $2.16 billion.

### Step 4: Discount everything to today

Discount the five forecast cash flows and terminal value back to the present. In this simplified example, their combined present value is about $1.73 billion. This is an estimate of enterprise value if the cash flow was calculated before payments to debt and equity providers.

### Step 5: Move from enterprise value to equity value

> Equity value = enterprise value + cash − debt − other senior claims

If the company has $200 million in cash and $300 million in debt, estimated equity value is $1.63 billion. With 50 million diluted shares, estimated value is about $32.60 per share.

This example omits many real-world adjustments, but it shows the logic behind a DCF. You can test assumptions with our [stock valuation tool](/valuation).

## Method 2: Comparable-company multiples

Relative valuation compares a company with similar businesses. Common multiples include:

- **Price-to-earnings, or P/E:** Share price divided by earnings per share.
- **Enterprise value to EBITDA:** Company value relative to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization.
- **Price-to-sales:** Market capitalization divided by revenue.
- **Price-to-free-cash-flow:** Market capitalization divided by free cash flow.

Suppose comparable companies trade at a median forward P/E of 20. If your company is expected to earn $3 per diluted share next year:

> Estimated share value = $3 forward EPS × 20 P/E = $60

This method is fast and reflects current market pricing, but “comparable” companies are never identical. Growth, margins, debt, cyclicality, accounting quality, and competitive position can justify very different multiples.

A sector can also be collectively overvalued or undervalued. Relative valuation tells you how the market prices peers, not necessarily what intrinsic value is.

## Method 3: Dividend discount model

The dividend discount model, or DDM, is most useful for mature companies with stable and predictable dividends.

For a dividend expected to grow at a constant rate:

> Value per share = next year’s dividend ÷ (required return − dividend growth rate)

If next year’s dividend is expected to be $2.10, your required return is 9%, and long-term dividend growth is 4%:

> Estimated value = $2.10 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = $42

The formula becomes unstable when the growth rate approaches the required return. It is also a poor fit for companies that do not pay dividends or whose payout changes unpredictably.

## Which method should you use?

Use the method that matches the business:

- DCF for companies with reasonably forecastable cash flows.
- Comparable multiples as a market-based cross-check.
- DDM for stable dividend payers.
- Asset value for businesses whose balance-sheet assets drive economics, such as some financial or real-estate companies.

Using two or more methods exposes assumptions that one model can hide.

## Build a valuation range

Create bear, base, and bull cases rather than forcing one forecast:

- **Bear case:** Slower growth, lower margins, or a higher discount rate.
- **Base case:** Your most defensible operating assumptions.
- **Bull case:** Stronger execution without assuming perfection.

Then compare the range with the market price. The gap between estimated value and purchase price is often called a margin of safety. It is a buffer for uncertainty, not protection against loss.

## Common valuation mistakes

1. **Confusing a low share price with a cheap company.** Always account for share count.
2. **Ignoring dilution.** Stock-based compensation can reduce each existing share’s claim.
3. **Using unrealistic perpetual growth.** No company can outgrow the economy forever.
4. **Mixing enterprise and equity metrics.** Compare enterprise value with operating metrics and equity value with shareholder earnings.
5. **Treating adjusted earnings as cash.** Reconcile profits with the cash-flow statement.
6. **Relying on false precision.** A model with many decimals is not necessarily accurate.
7. **Ignoring cyclicality.** Peak commodity or economic-cycle earnings can make a stock look deceptively cheap.

Before valuing a company, understand its [income statement, balance sheet, and cash-flow statement](/blog/how-to-read-financial-statements).

## The bottom line

Stock valuation translates assumptions about a business into an estimated value per share. DCF models focus on future cash, multiples compare market pricing, and dividend models focus on distributions. None eliminates uncertainty. Use conservative assumptions, test a range of outcomes, and update the work when the facts change.
