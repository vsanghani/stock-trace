An **exchange-traded fund**, or ETF, is an investment fund that holds a collection of assets and trades on an exchange throughout the day. Buying one ETF share can give you exposure to dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of securities.

An ETF is not automatically diversified or low risk. Its holdings determine its behavior. A broad global-stock ETF and a leveraged single-industry ETF are both ETFs, but they have very different risk profiles.

## How does an ETF work?

An ETF pools money from investors and follows a stated strategy. It might track a market index, select dividend-paying companies, hold government bonds, or focus on a commodity or industry.

Large financial institutions called **authorized participants** can create or redeem blocks of ETF shares by exchanging them for baskets of underlying assets. This mechanism helps keep the ETF’s market price close to the value of its holdings.

Investors generally buy and sell ETF shares through a brokerage account, just as they trade individual [stocks](/blog/what-is-a-stock).

## Index ETFs and actively managed ETFs

**Index ETFs** seek to track a benchmark. A fund following a broad-market index normally holds the index’s securities in similar proportions. Its goal is to approximate the benchmark’s return after costs—not beat it.

**Actively managed ETFs** give a portfolio manager discretion to select investments. They may respond more freely to changing conditions, but often charge higher fees and can underperform their benchmark.

Read the fund’s prospectus and holdings rather than relying on its name. Two funds with similar labels may use different indexes, weighting methods, or eligibility rules.

## ETF versus individual stock

An individual stock represents ownership in one company. An ETF share represents an interest in a portfolio.

An ETF can reduce the damage caused by one company failing because each holding may be only a small part of the fund. However, a concentrated sector ETF can still decline sharply if that entire industry struggles.

Individual stocks give investors control over company selection and position size. They also require more research and expose the portfolio to more company-specific risk.

## ETF versus mutual fund

Both structures pool investor money, but they trade differently:

- ETFs trade throughout the market day at changing prices.
- Traditional mutual funds process purchases and redemptions at end-of-day net asset value.
- ETFs can use limit orders; mutual funds generally cannot.
- Minimum investments, tax treatment, and automatic-investing features can differ.

Neither structure is universally better. Availability, strategy, taxes, costs, and investor behavior matter more than the label.

## Important ETF costs

### Expense ratio

The expense ratio is the fund’s annual operating cost as a percentage of assets. A 0.20% expense ratio costs approximately $20 per year for every $10,000 invested, though the fee is reflected in fund performance rather than billed separately.

Small fee differences can compound over long periods, but cost should be compared only after confirming that funds provide similar exposure.

### Bid-ask spread

ETF buyers pay the ask and sellers receive the bid. The gap is an implicit trading cost. Thinly traded or specialized funds may have wider spreads.

### Premium or discount

An ETF can trade above or below its net asset value. The creation and redemption process usually keeps the difference small in liquid markets, but gaps can widen during market stress or when underlying markets are closed.

### Brokerage and tax costs

Commissions may be zero at many brokers, but taxes, foreign withholding, currency conversion, and payment-for-order-flow arrangements can still affect results.

## Common types of ETFs

- **Broad-market stock ETFs:** Hold companies across sectors or countries.
- **Bond ETFs:** Hold government, municipal, or corporate debt.
- **Sector ETFs:** Focus on industries such as energy, technology, or healthcare.
- **Factor ETFs:** Select securities using characteristics such as value, size, quality, or momentum.
- **Commodity ETFs:** Track commodities through physical holdings, futures, or related securities.
- **Thematic ETFs:** Target trends such as robotics or clean energy.
- **Leveraged and inverse ETFs:** Seek a multiple or opposite of a benchmark’s *daily* return and can behave unexpectedly over longer periods.

## How to evaluate an ETF

Before investing, examine:

1. **Objective:** What is the fund designed to do?
2. **Holdings:** Which assets actually drive its results?
3. **Concentration:** How much sits in its largest companies, sectors, or countries?
4. **Index method:** Is it market-cap weighted, equal weighted, or rules based?
5. **Total cost:** Consider the expense ratio, spread, taxes, and tracking difference.
6. **Liquidity:** Review both ETF trading and the liquidity of its underlying assets.
7. **Fund structure:** Physical, synthetic, leveraged, and commodity products introduce different risks.

## Are ETFs safe?

An ETF is a wrapper, not a safety rating. A short-term government-bond ETF will generally have different risks from an emerging-market stock ETF. ETF investors can lose money due to market declines, interest-rate changes, credit defaults, currency moves, leverage, or tracking problems.

[Diversification](/blog/diversification-and-portfolio-risk) can reduce specific risks, but it cannot guarantee a profit or prevent broad-market losses.

## The bottom line

ETFs make it convenient to buy a portfolio through one tradable security. Their usefulness depends on what they hold, how they are constructed, and what they cost. Look through the wrapper before deciding whether an ETF fits your goals, risk tolerance, and time horizon.
