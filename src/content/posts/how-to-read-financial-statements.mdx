Financial statements describe what a company earned, what it owns and owes, and how cash moved during a reporting period. Investors typically focus on three connected reports: the income statement, balance sheet, and statement of cash flows.

No single number tells the whole story. Read the statements together, compare several years, and use the accompanying footnotes.

## The income statement: performance over a period

The income statement reports revenue, expenses, and profit over a quarter or year.

Its basic structure is:

> Revenue − expenses = net income

Important lines include:

- **Revenue:** Sales generated from customers.
- **Cost of revenue:** Direct costs associated with providing goods or services.
- **Gross profit:** Revenue minus cost of revenue.
- **Operating expenses:** Costs such as research, marketing, and administration.
- **Operating income:** Profit from core operations before interest and taxes.
- **Net income:** Profit after all recognized expenses.
- **Earnings per share, or EPS:** Net income available to common shareholders divided by diluted average shares.

### Margins make comparisons easier

A margin expresses profit as a percentage of revenue:

> Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue

> Operating margin = operating income ÷ revenue

> Net margin = net income ÷ revenue

Margins can show whether a company has pricing power and operating leverage. Compare them over time and with companies that have similar business models.

Do not stop at revenue growth. A company can grow sales while destroying value if acquisition costs, stock compensation, or capital needs rise faster.

## The balance sheet: a snapshot in time

The balance sheet reports resources and financial obligations on a specific date.

Its core equation is:

> Assets = liabilities + shareholders’ equity

**Assets** include cash, receivables, inventory, property, investments, and intangible assets. **Liabilities** include supplier bills, deferred revenue, loans, and other obligations. **Shareholders’ equity** is the accounting residual after liabilities are subtracted from assets.

Questions worth asking include:

- Can cash and near-term assets cover upcoming obligations?
- Is debt increasing faster than earnings or cash flow?
- Is inventory growing much faster than sales?
- Are customers taking longer to pay?
- Does goodwill from acquisitions dominate assets?

### Useful balance-sheet ratios

> Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities

> Net debt = total debt − cash and cash equivalents

> Debt-to-equity = total debt ÷ shareholders’ equity

Ratios require context. Banks naturally carry financial liabilities, while subscription businesses may report large deferred-revenue balances. Compare like with like.

## The cash-flow statement: where cash came from and went

The cash-flow statement reconciles accounting profit with the change in cash. It has three sections:

1. **Operating activities:** Cash generated or consumed by the core business.
2. **Investing activities:** Purchases and sales of equipment, investments, and businesses.
3. **Financing activities:** Debt, share issuance, repurchases, and dividends.

Investors often calculate:

> Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditures

Free cash flow can be used to repay debt, repurchase shares, pay dividends, or reinvest. But it is not standardized under accounting rules, so check how a company defines adjusted versions.

## How the statements connect

The three reports are linked:

- Net income begins the operating section of an indirect cash-flow statement.
- Depreciation reduces income but is added back because it is non-cash in the current period.
- Capital expenditures reduce cash and increase property and equipment on the balance sheet.
- New debt increases cash and liabilities.
- Share repurchases reduce cash and shareholders’ equity.
- Ending cash on the cash-flow statement should match balance-sheet cash.

Following one transaction through all three statements is an effective way to understand accounting.

## Earnings are not the same as cash flow

Accrual accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, not necessarily when cash moves. This creates legitimate timing differences—and opportunities for aggressive assumptions.

For example, a company may report revenue before collecting customer cash. The income statement shows sales, while the balance sheet records accounts receivable. If receivables repeatedly grow faster than revenue, investigate why.

Likewise, stock-based compensation is a non-cash expense in the current period, but issuing shares can dilute existing owners. Adding it back without considering dilution overstates economics.

## Five trends to review

Look at at least three to five years when possible:

1. Revenue growth and its source: volume, price, acquisitions, or currency.
2. Gross and operating margin direction.
3. Net income compared with operating cash flow.
4. Debt, interest expense, and repayment capacity.
5. Diluted share count and stock-based compensation.

Quarterly results can be seasonal. Compare a quarter with the same quarter one year earlier, not only the immediately preceding period.

## Red flags that deserve investigation

- Receivables or inventory consistently outpacing revenue
- Frequent “one-time” adjustments
- Positive net income but persistently negative operating cash flow
- Heavy borrowing to fund dividends or repurchases
- Rising share count despite headline buyback announcements
- Major changes in accounting policies or auditor relationships
- Large related-party transactions

A red flag is a prompt for research, not automatic proof of wrongdoing.

## Where to find reliable statements

For U.S.-listed companies, audited annual reports are filed on Form 10-K and quarterly reports on Form 10-Q through the SEC’s EDGAR database. Other markets have their own regulators and exchange filing systems. Investor-relations presentations are useful, but regulatory filings and their footnotes should be the primary source.

## The bottom line

The income statement measures accounting performance, the balance sheet shows financial position, and the cash-flow statement explains cash movement. Strong analysis connects all three, studies trends, reads footnotes, and reconciles reported profit with the cash and dilution experienced by shareholders.
