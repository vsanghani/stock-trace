A **stock** is a security that represents partial ownership of a company. If a company has one million shares outstanding and you own 10,000 of them, you own 1% of that company’s equity. That does not mean you can take 1% of its offices or inventory; it means you have a financial claim on part of the business and, depending on the share class, voting rights.

“Stock” usually describes ownership in one or more companies, while a “share” is one individual unit of that ownership. In everyday conversation, the terms are often used interchangeably.

## Why do companies issue stock?

Companies need capital to hire employees, develop products, buy equipment, acquire competitors, and enter new markets. A company can borrow money or sell part of its ownership to investors.

Selling stock can raise money without creating loan payments. The trade-off is dilution: existing owners hold a smaller percentage after new shares are issued. A private company can sell shares through an initial public offering, or IPO, and become publicly traded on an exchange such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq.

## How can a shareholder make money?

Stock investors generally seek returns in two ways:

1. **Price appreciation.** If you buy one share for $50 and later sell it for $65, your capital gain is $15 before taxes and transaction costs.
2. **Dividends.** Some companies distribute part of their profits to shareholders. A company paying $2 in annual dividends on a $50 share has a 4% dividend yield. See [how dividends work](/blog/how-dividends-work) for payment dates, yield, and payout risk.

Your total return combines price movement and cash distributions:

> Total return = (ending price − starting price + dividends) ÷ starting price

If a $50 stock rises to $54 and pays a $2 dividend, its total return is 12%: ($54 − $50 + $2) ÷ $50.

Returns are not guaranteed. The market price can fall, dividends can be reduced, and a failed business can leave common shareholders with nothing.

## Common stock and preferred stock

**Common stock** is what most people mean when discussing stocks. Common shareholders may vote on directors and major corporate matters. They participate in the company’s upside, but rank behind lenders and preferred shareholders if the business is liquidated.

**Preferred stock** behaves more like a blend of stock and a bond. It commonly pays a stated dividend and has priority over common stock for dividends and liquidation proceeds. Preferred shareholders usually have limited voting rights, and their upside may be capped.

Companies can also create multiple share classes. One class may carry more votes per share than another even though both represent economic ownership in the same business.

## What determines a stock’s market price?

A stock’s quoted price is the price at which buyers and sellers currently agree to trade. It changes as investors update their expectations about:

- Revenue, profit, and cash-flow growth
- Interest rates and inflation
- Competitive advantages and industry conditions
- Management decisions and capital allocation
- Regulation, economic data, and market sentiment
- The risk that expected results will not occur

Price alone does not tell you whether a company is cheap or expensive. A $500 share can represent a smaller company than a $20 share because the number of shares outstanding differs. [Market capitalization](/blog/market-cap-vs-stock-price) is the better starting point for comparing company size.

## Price is not the same as value

Market price is observable; intrinsic value is an estimate. Investors use methods such as discounted cash flow, comparable-company multiples, and dividend models to estimate what a business may be worth. Those methods depend on assumptions, so thoughtful investors use a range rather than pretending there is one perfectly precise answer.

Our guide to [calculating a stock’s value](/blog/how-to-value-a-stock) explains these approaches with examples.

## What are the main risks?

- **Business risk:** Sales, margins, or strategy may deteriorate.
- **Valuation risk:** A good company can still be a poor investment if the purchase price assumes unrealistic growth.
- **Market risk:** Broad economic or financial shocks can pull down many stocks at once.
- **Liquidity risk:** Thinly traded shares can be difficult to sell at the expected price.
- **Concentration risk:** One company or industry can dominate a portfolio’s outcome.
- **Currency and political risk:** International investments add exchange-rate and country-specific uncertainty.

Diversification can reduce company-specific risk, but it cannot eliminate losses.

## Stocks, ETFs, and mutual funds

Buying an individual stock gives you direct exposure to one company. An [exchange-traded fund, or ETF](/blog/what-is-an-etf), holds a basket of investments and trades throughout the day like a stock. A mutual fund also pools investors’ money, but typically processes trades once daily at net asset value.

Broad-market funds can provide instant diversification. Individual stocks offer more control and more company-specific upside and downside. The right choice depends on goals, time horizon, risk tolerance, and willingness to research businesses.

## A practical checklist before buying

Before making an investment decision, be able to explain:

1. How the company makes money.
2. Whether revenue, profit, and free cash flow are durable.
3. How much debt the company carries.
4. What could make the investment thesis wrong.
5. How the current valuation compares with realistic future results.
6. Why this position belongs in your broader portfolio.

Learning to read the [three financial statements](/blog/how-to-read-financial-statements) and the most common terms in our [investing glossary](/blog/investing-glossary) will give you a stronger foundation.

## The bottom line

A stock is a fractional ownership interest in a real business, not merely a symbol moving on a screen. Its long-term outcome is tied to the cash the business can generate and the price you pay for that claim. Understand both before putting capital at risk.
