**Free cash flow**, or FCF, estimates the cash a business generates after funding the capital expenditures needed for its operations. Investors follow it because cash can be reinvested, used to repay debt, distributed as dividends, or spent on repurchases and acquisitions.

Free cash flow is useful, but it is not formally standardized like line items defined by accounting rules. Always check how it was calculated.

## The common free cash flow formula

> Free cash flow = cash flow from operations − capital expenditures

If a company reports $500 million in operating cash flow and spends $180 million purchasing property and equipment:

> $500 million − $180 million = $320 million free cash flow

Both inputs usually appear on the statement of cash flows. Companies may label capital expenditures as purchases of property, plant and equipment.

## Free cash flow versus net income

Net income is calculated under accrual accounting. Revenue and expenses can be recognized before or after cash changes hands.

Operating cash flow starts with net income under the indirect method and adjusts for:

- Depreciation and amortization
- Stock-based compensation
- Deferred taxes
- Accounts receivable
- Inventory
- Accounts payable
- Other non-cash items and working-capital changes

Capital expenditures are then subtracted to calculate common FCF.

A company can report positive net income but negative free cash flow if it spends heavily on equipment or has not collected customer payments. Another can report FCF above net income because depreciation exceeds current capital spending or customers pay in advance.

## Why investors use free cash flow

### It measures financial flexibility

Recurring FCF can fund growth, reduce debt, and return capital without relying on new borrowing or share issuance.

### It supports valuation

Discounted cash-flow models estimate the present value of future cash. Our [stock valuation guide](/blog/how-to-value-a-stock) provides a worked example.

### It tests earnings quality

If reported profit rises for years while cash generation does not, investors should identify the reason.

### It helps assess distributions

Comparing dividends and buybacks with FCF indicates whether shareholder returns are funded internally.

## Free cash flow yield

> Free cash flow yield = free cash flow ÷ market capitalization

A company generating $500 million in FCF with a $10 billion market cap has a 5% FCF yield.

The inverse is price-to-free-cash-flow:

> Price-to-FCF = market capitalization ÷ free cash flow

In this example, price-to-FCF is 20.

These figures are not expected returns. Cash flow can fall, and management may allocate it poorly. Compare companies with similar economics and normalize unusual periods.

## Free cash flow to the firm and to equity

Professional valuation distinguishes two related measures.

**Free cash flow to the firm**, or FCFF, represents cash available to debt and equity providers before interest payments. It is discounted using a weighted average cost of capital to estimate enterprise value.

**Free cash flow to equity**, or FCFE, represents cash available to common shareholders after debt-related cash flows. It is discounted using the required return on equity to estimate equity value.

Do not discount one type of cash flow with the rate intended for the other.

## Growth spending complicates interpretation

Capital expenditures can maintain existing operations or expand capacity. Financial statements normally do not cleanly separate the two.

A growing company may have low current FCF because it is building profitable future capacity. A declining company may temporarily show strong FCF by cutting investment below the level needed to sustain operations.

Ask:

- What portion of spending maintains the existing business?
- What returns have past growth projects produced?
- Can management reduce spending without damaging competitiveness?
- Are assets being leased instead of purchased?

## Working-capital swings

Changes in receivables, inventory, payables, and deferred revenue can make one year’s FCF unrepresentative.

For example, delaying supplier payments increases current operating cash flow but cannot continue indefinitely. Collecting annual customer subscriptions upfront boosts cash before all associated revenue is recognized.

Review several years and understand the business cycle.

## Stock-based compensation

Stock-based compensation is added back in the operating cash-flow reconciliation because it does not use current cash. However, issuing shares transfers part of the company to employees and can dilute owners.

Treating the add-back as entirely free overstates shareholder economics. Consider both reported FCF and changes in diluted share count.

## Common warning signs

- “Adjusted FCF” excludes recurring cash costs.
- Receivables or unpaid bills create a temporary cash boost.
- Capital spending is cut below a sustainable level.
- Acquisitions replace internal investment but are excluded from the calculation.
- FCF is positive only because stock compensation replaces cash wages.
- One strong quarter is annualized despite seasonality.

## A practical review

Calculate at least three to five years of:

1. Operating cash flow
2. Capital expenditures
3. Free cash flow
4. FCF margin: free cash flow divided by revenue
5. FCF per diluted share
6. Dividends and repurchases relative to FCF

Explain large changes before projecting them forward.

## The bottom line

Free cash flow estimates the cash left after operating needs and capital expenditures. It is a valuable bridge between accounting results and financial capacity, but one period can be distorted by working capital, underinvestment, or adjustments. Study the calculation, trend, and per-share result before using FCF in valuation.
