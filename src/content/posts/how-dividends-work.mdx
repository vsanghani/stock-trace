A **dividend** is a distribution from a company to its shareholders. Most stock dividends are paid in cash, although companies can also distribute additional shares or other assets.

Dividends are one part of an investor’s total return. They are not guaranteed interest payments: a company’s board can increase, reduce, suspend, or eliminate them.

## Why do companies pay dividends?

Mature businesses may generate more cash than they can reinvest at attractive returns. Management can use that excess cash to repay debt, acquire companies, repurchase shares, or pay dividends.

Fast-growing companies often retain earnings to expand. That does not make them inferior, just as paying a dividend does not automatically make a company financially strong. The relevant question is whether management allocates capital in a way that increases long-term value per share.

## The four important dividend dates

1. **Declaration date:** The board announces the dividend amount and schedule.
2. **Ex-dividend date:** Buyers on or after this date generally do not receive the upcoming dividend.
3. **Record date:** The company identifies shareholders entitled to payment.
4. **Payment date:** Cash or shares are distributed.

Settlement rules determine the relationship between the ex-dividend and record dates and can change. Verify dates with the company, exchange, or broker.

## What happens to the stock price?

On the ex-dividend date, a stock’s price will theoretically fall by approximately the dividend amount, all else equal. A $50 company paying a $1 dividend has transferred $1 per share of value from corporate cash to shareholders.

Actual market movement can be larger, smaller, or in the opposite direction because other news and trading affect the price at the same time. A dividend is not free money.

## How to calculate dividend yield

> Dividend yield = annual dividends per share ÷ current share price

If a stock pays $0.50 quarterly, annualized dividends are $2. At a $40 share price:

> $2 ÷ $40 = 5% dividend yield

Trailing yield uses dividends already paid. Forward yield usually annualizes the latest declared payment. If the dividend changes, the actual future yield will differ.

Yield also moves when share price moves. A stock falling from $40 to $20 would show a 10% yield if the $2 dividend were maintained—but the decline may signal that investors expect a cut.

## What is the payout ratio?

The payout ratio compares dividends with the resources available to fund them:

> Earnings payout ratio = dividends per share ÷ earnings per share

> Cash payout ratio = total dividends ÷ free cash flow

A company earning $4 per share and paying $2 has a 50% earnings payout ratio.

There is no ideal ratio for every industry. Stable utilities may support higher payouts than cyclical manufacturers. Real estate investment trusts also operate under special distribution rules. Compare a company with its own history and relevant peers.

Ratios above 100% can be temporary, but they are not sustainable indefinitely without cash reserves, borrowing, asset sales, or improved results.

## Dividend growth matters

A lower current yield with durable growth can produce more future income than a high yield that stagnates or gets cut.

When assessing dividend growth, ask:

- Are revenue and cash flow growing?
- Is the payout ratio stable?
- Can debt and interest obligations be met?
- Does the company need heavy capital spending?
- How did the dividend behave during previous downturns?
- Is share count rising enough to make total dividend costs grow faster than per-share figures suggest?

Use audited filings and our guide to [reading financial statements](/blog/how-to-read-financial-statements) to answer these questions.

## Dividend reinvestment

A dividend reinvestment plan, or DRIP, uses distributions to purchase additional shares. Reinvestment can compound returns because future dividends are paid on a growing share count.

Suppose 100 shares pay $2 annually. The $200 distribution buys five more shares at $40. If the dividend remains $2, the next annual payment would be $210 on 105 shares.

Reinvestment does not remove risk. It increases exposure to the same investment, and taxes may be owed even when cash is automatically reinvested.

## Cash dividends versus share repurchases

Dividends distribute cash directly and treat each share equally. Repurchases reduce shares outstanding when the company buys its own stock.

Buybacks can create value when shares are purchased below intrinsic value and can destroy value when management overpays. They can also offset dilution from employee compensation without meaningfully reducing total share count.

Dividends are more visible and can attract income-focused investors, but they are less flexible because markets often punish cuts.

## Beware of dividend traps

An unusually high yield may indicate distress rather than opportunity. Warning signs include:

- Falling free cash flow
- Rising debt and interest expense
- A payout ratio consistently above sustainable levels
- A shrinking or highly cyclical business
- Funding distributions through asset sales or new borrowing
- Management guidance that implies weaker results

Do not evaluate yield in isolation. Estimate the company’s financial resilience and [stock value](/blog/how-to-value-a-stock).

## Taxes and account rules

Dividend taxation varies by country, holding period, account type, and whether a payment is classified as qualified, ordinary, or a return of capital. International shares can involve withholding taxes. Consult current local rules or a qualified tax professional.

## The bottom line

Dividends turn part of a company’s cash into shareholder income. Focus on the durability of the business and payout—not merely the displayed yield. A sustainable dividend is supported by recurring cash flow, a manageable balance sheet, and disciplined capital allocation.
