Stock analysis is the process of deciding what a business may earn, what could go wrong, and whether the market price offers an attractive balance of potential return and risk.

A repeatable process is more useful than chasing isolated tips or ratios. The following framework moves from understanding the company to valuing its shares.

## 1. Understand the business model

Start by explaining the company in plain language:

- What does it sell?
- Who are its customers?
- Why do customers choose it?
- How does it charge?
- Which products, regions, or customers generate most revenue?
- Which costs are fixed and which vary with sales?

If you cannot explain how the company makes money, its financial results will be difficult to forecast.

Read the latest annual report, especially the business, risk-factor, and segment sections. Investor presentations can provide context, but use regulatory filings as the primary source.

## 2. Evaluate the industry

A strong operator can still struggle in an unattractive industry. Consider:

- Market size and realistic growth
- Number and strength of competitors
- Customer and supplier bargaining power
- Regulation and technological change
- Sensitivity to interest rates, commodities, or the economic cycle
- Threat of substitute products

Be skeptical of broad market-size estimates that include revenue a company cannot realistically address.

## 3. Identify the competitive advantage

A durable competitive advantage allows a company to defend customers or attractive economics. Potential advantages include:

- Network effects
- High switching costs
- Low-cost production
- Trusted brands
- Patents or regulatory licenses
- Unique data or distribution
- Economies of scale

An advantage should appear in evidence such as customer retention, pricing power, market share, margins, or return on invested capital. A management claim is not enough.

## 4. Study financial trends

Review at least three to five years when available. Focus on:

- Revenue growth and whether it is organic
- Gross and operating margins
- Operating and free cash flow
- Return on invested capital
- Debt and interest coverage
- Stock-based compensation
- Diluted share count

Connect the income statement, balance sheet, and statement of cash flows. Our [financial-statement guide](/blog/how-to-read-financial-statements) explains how.

Quality growth converts into cash without requiring disproportionate debt, dilution, or capital spending.

## 5. Assess the balance sheet

Financial resilience matters most when conditions worsen. Compare:

- Cash with near-term obligations
- Total and net debt with cash flow
- Interest expense with operating profit
- Debt maturity dates
- Inventory and receivables with sales
- Pension, lease, and other contractual commitments

A company with manageable leverage has more options during a downturn. A heavily indebted company may be forced to issue shares, sell assets, or cut investment at the worst time.

## 6. Examine management and incentives

Management allocates shareholder capital. Review its record of:

- Meeting or missing stated objectives
- Reinvesting in the core business
- Acquisitions and divestitures
- Debt management
- Share issuance and repurchases
- Dividend decisions

Read the proxy statement to understand executive compensation. Incentives tied only to revenue or adjusted profit can encourage growth without adequate regard for cash, risk, or dilution.

Insider ownership can align leaders with shareholders, but concentration can also weaken governance. Evaluate the full structure.

## 7. Create a simple forecast

Forecast the drivers rather than copying a headline growth rate:

> Revenue = customers × average revenue per customer

> Operating profit = revenue × operating margin

> Free cash flow ≈ operating cash flow − capital expenditures

For a retailer, store count and same-store sales may matter. For software, customer additions, retention, and contract value may matter. Keep assumptions visible and connect them with business reality.

Build bear, base, and bull scenarios. The bear case should describe a plausible adverse outcome, not merely slightly slower growth.

## 8. Estimate value

Use at least two relevant methods:

- Discounted cash flow
- P/E or price-to-free-cash-flow
- Enterprise value to EBITDA or revenue
- Dividend discount model
- Asset or sum-of-the-parts value

Our guides explain the [P/E ratio](/blog/pe-ratio-explained) and [three practical stock-valuation methods](/blog/how-to-value-a-stock).

Compare the current price with a valuation range. A margin of safety can help account for forecast error, but it cannot eliminate risk.

## 9. Write the risks before buying

List what would invalidate the investment thesis:

- A competitor taking share
- Customer concentration
- Margin compression
- Regulation
- Refinancing needs
- Technological disruption
- Management turnover
- Valuation depending on unrealistic growth

Estimate impact and probability where possible. Also identify indicators you will monitor. “The price went down” is not itself evidence that the thesis failed; deteriorating business facts may be.

## 10. Decide portfolio fit

An attractive company is not automatically an appropriate position. Consider:

- Existing exposure to the same industry or risk factor
- Time horizon and liquidity needs
- Maximum tolerable loss
- Position size
- Tax consequences
- More attractive alternatives

[Diversification](/blog/diversification-and-portfolio-risk) addresses portfolio-level risk that company analysis cannot remove.

## A concise stock research checklist

Before investing, document:

1. The business in one paragraph.
2. Its primary competitive advantage.
3. Five-year financial trends.
4. Balance-sheet strengths and weaknesses.
5. Management’s capital-allocation record.
6. Bear, base, and bull assumptions.
7. A valuation range using at least two methods.
8. Three reasons the thesis could fail.
9. The position’s role in the portfolio.
10. Which facts would cause you to sell or reassess.

## The bottom line

Good stock analysis connects business quality, financial evidence, valuation, and portfolio risk. It does not require predicting every quarter. It requires making assumptions explicit, checking them against new information, and refusing to confuse a compelling story with a sound investment.
