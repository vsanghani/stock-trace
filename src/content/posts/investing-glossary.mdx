Financial language can make simple ideas seem complicated. This glossary defines common stock-market and investing terms in plain English. Definitions are educational summaries; legal, accounting, and broker-specific meanings can be more precise.

## A

**Ask price:** The lowest price at which a seller is currently willing to sell a security.

**Asset allocation:** How a portfolio is divided among asset classes such as stocks, bonds, and cash.

**Average cost:** The total amount invested in a position divided by the number of shares owned.

## B

**Bear market:** A substantial, sustained market decline, commonly—but not universally—described as a fall of at least 20% from a recent high.

**Beta:** A historical measure of how sensitively an investment has moved relative to a benchmark. A beta above 1 indicates greater past sensitivity, not guaranteed future volatility.

**Bid price:** The highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay for a security.

**Bid-ask spread:** The difference between the highest bid and lowest ask. Wider spreads usually mean higher trading friction.

**Bond:** A debt security through which an investor lends money to a government, company, or other issuer in exchange for promised payments.

**Bull market:** A substantial, sustained period of rising market prices.

## C

**Call option:** A contract giving its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy an underlying asset at a set strike price. See [our options guide](/blog/what-are-stock-options).

**Capital gain or loss:** The difference between an asset’s sale price and its tax basis, subject to local tax rules.

**Compound return:** Growth earned on both original capital and previously accumulated gains or income.

**Correlation:** A measure of how two investments have historically moved in relation to each other, ranging from −1 to +1.

## D

**Derivative:** A contract whose value depends on an underlying asset, rate, or index. Options and futures are derivatives.

**Dilution:** Reduction in an existing shareholder’s ownership percentage or per-share claim caused by an increase in shares.

**Discount rate:** The rate used to translate future cash flows into present value, reflecting time and risk.

**Diversification:** Spreading exposure among investments to reduce reliance on a single outcome. It cannot prevent all losses.

**Dividend:** Cash or stock distributed by a company to shareholders. Dividends can be reduced or eliminated.

**Dividend yield:** Annual dividends per share divided by current share price.

**Dollar-cost averaging:** Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule, resulting in more shares bought at lower prices and fewer at higher prices.

## E

**Earnings per share, or EPS:** Profit available to common shareholders divided by weighted-average shares, usually shown on a basic and diluted basis.

**EBITDA:** Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It is a non-GAAP proxy for operating performance, not cash flow.

**Enterprise value, or EV:** Market capitalization plus debt and other senior claims, minus cash. It estimates the value of the operating business across capital providers.

**ETF:** An exchange-traded fund that holds a basket of assets and trades on an exchange throughout the day.

## F

**Free cash flow:** Cash from operations minus capital expenditures under a common definition. Company-specific adjusted definitions may differ.

**Fundamental analysis:** Evaluating a security using business, industry, financial, and valuation information.

## I

**Implied volatility:** The level of expected volatility embedded in an option’s market price.

**Index:** A rules-based measure tracking a group of securities, such as the S&P 500. An index itself generally cannot be purchased directly, though funds can track it.

**Initial public offering, or IPO:** The process through which a private company first offers shares to public investors.

**Intrinsic value:** An estimate of an asset’s underlying economic worth. For an option, it specifically means the amount the contract is in the money.

## L

**Limit order:** An instruction to buy no higher than, or sell no lower than, a specified price. Execution is not guaranteed.

**Liquidity:** How readily an asset can be traded without materially affecting its price.

## M

**Margin:** Borrowed money used to purchase securities; the term can also refer to profit as a percentage of revenue. Context matters.

**Margin of safety:** The gap between an investor’s estimated value and purchase price, intended to allow for uncertainty.

**Market capitalization:** Share price multiplied by shares outstanding. Read [market cap versus share price](/blog/market-cap-vs-stock-price).

**Market order:** An instruction to trade promptly at the best available price. Execution is likely in liquid markets, but the price is not guaranteed.

**Mutual fund:** A pooled investment vehicle generally priced once per trading day at net asset value.

## O

**Option premium:** The market price paid by an option buyer and received by its seller.

## P

**P/E ratio:** Share price divided by earnings per share, or market capitalization divided by total common earnings.

**Portfolio:** The collection of investments owned by a person or institution.

**Put option:** A contract giving its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to sell an underlying asset at a set strike price.

## R

**Rebalancing:** Returning a portfolio toward target allocations by buying, selling, or directing new contributions.

**Return on equity, or ROE:** Net income divided by average shareholders’ equity. High leverage can increase this ratio.

**Risk tolerance:** An investor’s financial and emotional capacity to accept uncertain outcomes and losses.

## S

**Share:** One unit of ownership in a company or fund. Learn more in [What Is a Stock?](/blog/what-is-a-stock).

**Short selling:** Borrowing a security and selling it in anticipation of buying it back at a lower price. Losses can exceed the initial proceeds.

**Stock split:** An increase in share count with a proportional reduction in price per share. A split does not directly change total company value.

**Strike price:** The price at which an option holder may buy or sell the underlying asset.

## T

**Ticker symbol:** A short identifier used to represent a publicly traded security on an exchange.

**Total return:** Price change plus distributions, divided by the initial investment value.

## V

**Valuation:** The process of estimating what an asset or business is worth. Explore [three stock valuation methods](/blog/how-to-value-a-stock).

**Volatility:** The degree to which returns or prices vary over time. Historical volatility describes the past; implied volatility is derived from option prices.

**Volume:** The number of shares or contracts traded during a period.

## Y

**Yield:** Income from an investment expressed as a percentage of price or value. The exact calculation depends on the asset.
