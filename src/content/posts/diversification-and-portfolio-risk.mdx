**Diversification** means spreading exposure across investments whose results are not driven by exactly the same risks. Its purpose is to reduce the damage that one company, industry, country, or economic outcome can cause to a portfolio.

Diversification cannot guarantee gains or prevent losses during a broad market decline. It changes the concentration of risk; it does not make risk disappear.

## Company-specific and market-wide risk

Investors face two broad categories of risk.

**Company-specific risk** includes a failed product, accounting problem, lawsuit, or loss of a major customer. Holding many independently exposed companies can reduce the effect of any one event.

**Systematic risk** affects much of the market. Recessions, interest-rate shocks, wars, and liquidity crises can pull many assets down together. Diversification within stocks alone cannot eliminate this risk.

## Why the number of holdings can mislead

Owning 30 stocks is not necessarily more diversified than owning 10. If all 30 are small technology companies dependent on low interest rates, their underlying risks overlap.

Look through ticker symbols to the economic drivers:

- Industry and business model
- Geography and currency
- Customer type
- Interest-rate sensitivity
- Commodity exposure
- Company size
- Growth versus value characteristics

Several ETFs may also hold the same largest companies. Count exposures, not fund names.

## Ways to diversify

### Across companies

Limiting the weight of any one company reduces the impact of company-specific failure. The appropriate limit depends on risk tolerance, liquidity, tax situation, and ability to monitor the position.

### Across industries

Banks, utilities, software companies, manufacturers, and healthcare businesses respond differently to economic changes. Sector diversification can reduce reliance on one profit cycle.

### Across asset classes

Stocks, government bonds, corporate bonds, cash, real estate, and commodities have different sources of return and risk. Their relationships are not stable, but combining them can make a portfolio less dependent on stock-market outcomes.

### Across countries and currencies

International exposure can reduce dependence on one economy. It introduces currency, political, accounting, and regulatory risks, so global does not mean risk free.

### Across time

Investing on a schedule can reduce the risk of committing all available capital at one unfortunate moment. Dollar-cost averaging does not ensure a profit and may underperform immediate investing when markets rise.

## Correlation matters

Correlation measures how two investments have historically moved relative to each other:

- +1 means they moved perfectly together.
- 0 means no consistent linear relationship.
- −1 means they moved perfectly opposite each other.

Combining less-correlated assets can reduce portfolio volatility. But historical correlations can change, especially in a crisis when investors sell many risky assets simultaneously.

Use correlation as evidence, not a permanent law. Our [correlation analysis tool](/correlation) can help visualize relationships among holdings.

## Asset allocation versus security selection

**Asset allocation** determines how much is assigned to broad groups such as stocks, bonds, and cash. **Security selection** determines which investments fill each group.

Allocation usually drives the portfolio’s overall sensitivity to growth, inflation, interest rates, and market declines. Picking several stocks inside a 100% equity allocation diversifies companies but does not diversify away from equity-market risk.

Your allocation should reflect:

- Financial goals
- Time until money is needed
- Income stability
- Emergency reserves
- Capacity and willingness to tolerate losses
- Tax and legal circumstances

## Rebalancing

Market movement causes allocations to drift. If stocks rise faster than bonds, a 60% stock target might become 70%.

Rebalancing returns the portfolio toward its intended risk:

- At a fixed interval, such as annually
- When an allocation crosses a chosen threshold
- By directing new contributions toward underweight assets

Trading can create taxes and costs. Avoid rebalancing so frequently that normal market noise drives unnecessary activity.

## Concentration is sometimes hidden

Review these common sources:

- Employer stock plus income from the same employer
- Home ownership and investments tied to one local economy
- Multiple funds tracking similar indexes
- Broad indexes dominated by a handful of companies
- Stocks with different labels but the same supplier or commodity dependence
- Options that increase exposure to an existing position

Calculate exposures after looking through funds and derivatives where data is available.

## Can a portfolio be over-diversified?

Adding an investment improves diversification only if it contributes a useful exposure at a reasonable cost. A very large number of overlapping holdings can:

- Make the portfolio difficult to understand
- Add fees and tax complexity
- Dilute well-researched ideas
- Produce benchmark-like results at higher cost
- Hide unintended duplication

The goal is not to maximize the number of positions. It is to build a portfolio in which no single avoidable risk controls the outcome.

## A simple diversification review

Ask:

1. What are the five largest positions?
2. How much is exposed to the largest sector and country?
3. Which holdings depend on the same economic conditions?
4. Could job income and investments decline together?
5. When will allocation be reviewed or rebalanced?
6. Is enough liquid cash available for near-term needs?
7. Are fees and taxes justified by each holding’s role?

Broad [ETFs](/blog/what-is-an-etf) can simplify diversification, but always inspect their holdings and construction. [Bonds](/blog/what-is-a-bond) and cash can change the portfolio’s sensitivity to equity [bull and bear markets](/blog/bull-vs-bear-market).

## The bottom line

Diversification reduces dependence on specific outcomes by combining genuinely different exposures. Build it around goals and risk capacity, look through overlapping holdings, and rebalance deliberately. A portfolio should be understandable enough that you know which risks you own and why.
