A **market order** prioritizes immediate execution at the best available price. A **limit order** sets the worst price you are willing to accept but may never execute.

The distinction is between price certainty and execution certainty. Neither order type guarantees both.

## How a market order works

A market order instructs a broker to trade promptly against available orders.

If a stock shows:

- Highest bid: $49.98
- Lowest ask: $50.02

A small market buy will likely execute near $50.02, while a small market sell will likely execute near $49.98. Prices can change before the order reaches the market.

A market order guarantees neither the displayed quote nor one execution price. Large orders may fill at several prices.

## How a limit order works

A buy limit sets the maximum price you will pay. A sell limit sets the minimum price you will accept.

- A buy limit at $49 can execute at $49 or lower.
- A sell limit at $51 can execute at $51 or higher.

If the market never reaches the limit—or other orders have priority—the trade does not occur.

## Price improvement

A limit price is a boundary, not necessarily the execution price. A buy limit at $50 can fill at $49.90 if that is the best available price. A sell limit at $50 can fill above $50.

Broker routing and local market rules influence execution quality. Review your broker’s disclosures.

## The bid-ask spread

The **bid** is the highest current buying price; the **ask** is the lowest current selling price. Their difference is the spread.

> Bid-ask spread = ask price − bid price

Using a $49.98 bid and $50.02 ask, the spread is $0.04.

Frequently traded securities tend to have narrower spreads than thinly traded stocks or [options](/blog/what-are-stock-options). Spreads can widen during volatile markets, near announcements, and outside regular trading hours.

## Slippage and market impact

**Slippage** is the difference between the expected and actual execution price.

Suppose only 100 shares are offered at $50.02, with another 400 at $50.10. A market order to buy 500 shares may receive both prices, creating an average above the quote first displayed.

The order itself can also move the market when it is large relative to available liquidity. This is market impact.

## When might a market order be appropriate?

A market order may be reasonable when:

- The security is highly liquid
- The order is small relative to normal trading
- The spread is narrow
- Immediate execution matters more than a small price difference
- Markets are operating normally

Even then, inspect the current quote and market conditions.

## When might a limit order be appropriate?

A limit order may be preferable when:

- The security trades infrequently
- The spread is wide
- Price is changing quickly
- You are trading outside regular hours
- The order is large relative to displayed liquidity
- You have a clear maximum purchase or minimum sale price

The cost of that control is non-execution. A stock can move away and never return.

## Stop and stop-limit orders

A **stop order** becomes a market order after a trigger price is reached. It can help automate an exit but may fill far from the stop during a gap or fast market.

A **stop-limit order** becomes a limit order after the trigger. It controls the worst accepted price but can remain unfilled while losses continue.

Broker definitions and trigger methods differ. Understand the exact instructions before using them.

## Day and good-til-canceled orders

A **day order** expires if it is not filled during that trading session.

A **good-til-canceled**, or GTC, order remains active until filled, canceled, or expired under the broker’s time limit.

Long-lived orders can execute after new information changes your view. Review open orders regularly.

## Extended-hours risks

Pre-market and after-hours sessions often have:

- Lower volume
- Wider spreads
- Fewer available prices
- Greater volatility
- Different order-handling rules

Many brokers accept only limit orders during these periods. Quotes from one venue may not represent the full market.

## Partial fills and queue priority

A limit order may be partly executed. If you place a buy for 1,000 shares and only 200 become available at your limit, you may receive 200 while the rest remains open.

Orders at the same price usually follow exchange priority rules, often involving time. A market touching your limit does not guarantee your order was first in line.

## Common order-entry mistakes

- Reversing buy and sell instructions
- Entering the wrong number of shares
- Misplacing a decimal in the limit price
- Ignoring an unusually wide spread
- Leaving stale GTC orders active
- Using market orders in illiquid securities
- Assuming a stop price is a guaranteed sale price
- Trading before understanding settlement and account rules

Review the confirmation screen and check the order status afterward. Cancel requests may arrive too late if execution has already occurred.

## The bottom line

Market orders prioritize execution but expose you to uncertain prices. Limit orders control price but expose you to missed trades. Choose based on liquidity, spread, volatility, urgency, and order size—not habit. Order type manages execution; it does not make the underlying [stock](/blog/what-is-a-stock) a good investment.
