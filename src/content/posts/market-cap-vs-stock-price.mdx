A low stock price does not necessarily mean a company is cheap, and a high stock price does not necessarily mean it is expensive. Share price tells you the price of one unit of ownership. **Market capitalization** tells you the market value of all common equity.

## How to calculate market capitalization

> Market capitalization = current share price × shares outstanding

Consider two fictional companies:

- Company A trades at $10 and has 5 billion shares. Its market cap is $50 billion.
- Company B trades at $500 and has 20 million shares. Its market cap is $10 billion.

Company A has the lower stock price but is five times larger by market capitalization. Looking at price without share count creates a misleading comparison.

## Why do share counts differ?

A company chooses an initial number of shares and can later change that count through:

- Issuing shares to raise capital
- Paying employees with stock
- Acquiring another business with shares
- Repurchasing shares
- Converting debt or preferred securities
- Stock splits and reverse stock splits

Investors should monitor **diluted shares outstanding**, which includes potential shares from instruments such as employee options and convertible securities. If profit stays flat while diluted share count rises, earnings per share decline.

## What happens in a stock split?

In a 10-for-1 split, an investor with one $1,000 share receives 10 shares worth roughly $100 each immediately after the split. The investor still owns $1,000 of stock, and the company’s market cap is unchanged.

The arithmetic is:

> Before: $1,000 × 1 share = $1,000

> After: $100 × 10 shares = $1,000

A reverse split works in the opposite direction. Neither action creates business value by itself, although market perception and liquidity can affect subsequent trading.

## Market cap is equity value, not total business value

Market cap represents the value attributed to common shareholders. **Enterprise value**, or EV, adjusts for the company’s financing:

> Enterprise value = market cap + total debt + preferred equity + minority interest − cash

Imagine a company with a $10 billion market cap, $4 billion of debt, and $1 billion in cash. Its simplified enterprise value is $13 billion.

This distinction matters when comparing companies with different capital structures. A buyer acquiring the entire business would effectively assume its debt and gain access to its cash.

## How investors use size categories

Market participants often group companies into broad categories:

- **Large-cap:** Typically $10 billion or more
- **Mid-cap:** Roughly $2 billion to $10 billion
- **Small-cap:** Roughly $300 million to $2 billion
- **Micro-cap:** Roughly $50 million to $300 million

These boundaries are conventions, not universal rules. Index providers use their own methodologies and update constituents as markets change.

Larger companies often have more diversified operations, deeper trading liquidity, and wider analyst coverage. Smaller companies may have more room to grow but can carry greater business, financing, and liquidity risk. Size alone does not determine quality or future return.

## Does market cap tell you whether a stock is expensive?

No. Market cap measures size, not value relative to business performance. To assess valuation, investors compare equity or enterprise value with financial measures:

- Market cap relative to earnings or free cash flow
- Enterprise value relative to revenue or EBITDA
- Market price relative to an estimate of discounted future cash flows

A $200 billion company can be undervalued if it produces durable cash flows that justify more. A $200 million company can be overvalued if expectations exceed realistic outcomes.

Size also says nothing about [earnings quality](/blog/earnings-per-share-explained). Our guide to [stock valuation methods](/blog/how-to-value-a-stock) explains how to make those comparisons.

## Market cap can change in two ways

Market capitalization changes when either the share price or share count changes:

1. **Price movement:** If investors bid the stock higher, market cap rises even without any new shares.
2. **Share issuance or repurchases:** Market cap can change as the company adds or removes shares, although the transaction’s use of cash and market response also matter.

This is why historical per-share data should be adjusted for splits and why investors should check changes in diluted share count over time.

## The bottom line

Share price is the cost of one share. Market cap is the market value of all common shares. Enterprise value expands the view to include debt, cash, and other claims. Use the right measure for the question, and never call a stock “cheap” solely because its quoted price is low.
