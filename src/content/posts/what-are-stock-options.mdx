A **stock option** is a contract that gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying stock at a specified price by a specified date. The seller takes the other side of that agreement and must perform if the buyer exercises.

Options are derivatives because their value is derived from another asset. They can be used to hedge risk, generate income, or speculate, but their expiration dates and leverage make them more complex than [owning shares](/blog/what-is-a-stock). Contract prices also depend on how orders are sent to the market; see [market orders versus limit orders](/blog/market-order-vs-limit-order).

## The two basic option types

A **call option** gives its holder the right to buy the underlying stock at the strike price. Call buyers generally benefit when the stock rises.

A **put option** gives its holder the right to sell the underlying stock at the strike price. Put buyers generally benefit when the stock falls.

In standard U.S. equity options, one contract usually controls 100 shares. A quoted premium of $2.50 therefore usually costs $250: $2.50 × 100 shares. Contract specifications can differ, especially after corporate actions, so always verify them.

## Five terms every options investor should know

1. **Underlying:** The stock or other security connected to the option.
2. **Strike price:** The price at which the holder may buy or sell the underlying.
3. **Expiration date:** The final date on which the contract remains valid.
4. **Premium:** The option’s market price, paid by the buyer to the seller.
5. **Exercise:** Using the contractual right to buy or sell at the strike.

An option is **in the money** when exercising it has immediate value. A $50-strike call is in the money when the stock trades above $50. A $50-strike put is in the money when the stock trades below $50.

## A call option example

Suppose a stock trades at $48. You buy one call with:

- A $50 strike price
- One month until expiration
- A premium of $2 per share, or $200 total

At expiration, the call’s value is:

> Call value = maximum of $0 or (stock price − strike price)

If the stock closes at $58, the contract has $8 per share of intrinsic value. After subtracting the $2 premium, the profit is $6 per share, or $600 before fees.

The break-even stock price at expiration is $52: the $50 strike plus the $2 premium. If the stock finishes at or below $50, the option expires worthless and the buyer loses the $200 premium.

## A put option example

Now suppose you buy a $50-strike put for $3 per share.

At expiration:

> Put value = maximum of $0 or (strike price − stock price)

If the stock closes at $40, the put is worth $10 per share. Subtracting the $3 premium produces a $7-per-share profit, or $700. The break-even price is $47: the $50 strike minus the $3 premium.

If the stock finishes at or above $50, the buyer can lose the entire $300 premium.

## Intrinsic value and time value

An option premium has up to two components:

- **Intrinsic value** is the amount the option is currently in the money.
- **Time value** is the extra amount buyers pay for the possibility of favorable movement before expiration.

Time value generally declines as expiration approaches, a process called **time decay**. This does not happen at a constant speed; decay often accelerates near expiration.

Options prices are also influenced by the underlying price, expected volatility, interest rates, dividends, and time remaining. Models such as Black-Scholes estimate theoretical value, but market supply and demand determine the traded premium.

## What are the Greeks?

The Greeks summarize how an option’s theoretical value responds to changing inputs:

- **Delta:** Estimated price change for a $1 move in the underlying.
- **Gamma:** How quickly delta changes.
- **Theta:** Estimated value lost as time passes, all else equal.
- **Vega:** Sensitivity to implied volatility.
- **Rho:** Sensitivity to interest rates.

Greeks are estimates, not guarantees. They change continuously with market conditions.

## Buying versus selling options

An option buyer pays a premium for a right. For a simple long call or long put, the maximum loss is generally the premium paid.

An option seller receives the premium but accepts an obligation. A covered call seller owns the underlying shares, which limits one form of delivery risk but caps upside. An uncovered call can create theoretically unlimited loss because the stock price has no fixed ceiling.

“Income” from selling options is not free yield. The premium compensates the seller for taking risk.

## Early exercise and assignment

American-style equity options may generally be exercised before expiration. Sellers can therefore be assigned at any time while the contract is open. Assignment risk can increase around ex-dividend dates and near expiration.

Some index options use European-style exercise and cash settlement. Product rules vary, so read the contract specifications rather than relying on assumptions.

## Major risks beginners overlook

- **Total premium loss:** A directionally correct idea can still lose if the move is too small or too late.
- **Leverage:** Small stock moves can create large percentage changes in an option.
- **Volatility changes:** An option can fall after an event even when the stock moves in the expected direction.
- **Wide bid-ask spreads:** Trading costs can be substantial in illiquid contracts.
- **Assignment:** Sellers may unexpectedly acquire or deliver shares.
- **Complex payoff profiles:** Multi-leg strategies introduce additional execution and management risk.

Before trading, model the maximum gain, maximum loss, break-even price, and effect of time. Use our [options analysis tools](/options) to explore contracts, but verify all data with your broker before placing an order.

## The bottom line

Options transfer risk between buyers and sellers. Calls provide a right to buy; puts provide a right to sell. The premium reflects time, volatility, and the underlying price—not just a prediction about direction. Understand the contract and worst-case outcome before using the leverage it provides.
