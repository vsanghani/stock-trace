The **price-to-earnings ratio**, or P/E, compares a company’s stock price with the profit attributable to each share. It is one of the most widely used valuation multiples—and one of the easiest to misuse.

## How to calculate P/E

There are two equivalent formulas:

> P/E ratio = share price ÷ earnings per share

> P/E ratio = market capitalization ÷ net income available to common shareholders

If a stock trades at $60 and earned $3 per diluted share over the past year:

> $60 ÷ $3 = 20 P/E

Investors are currently paying $20 for each dollar of trailing annual earnings. This does not mean it will take exactly 20 years to recover the investment because earnings, distributions, and valuation can change.

## Trailing P/E versus forward P/E

**Trailing P/E** normally uses reported earnings from the last 12 months. Its advantage is that the earnings occurred; its disadvantage is that they may no longer represent current conditions.

**Forward P/E** uses estimated earnings, commonly for the next fiscal year or next 12 months. It is more future-focused but only as reliable as the forecast.

A company trading at $60 with $3 in trailing EPS and an estimated $4 in forward EPS has:

- Trailing P/E of 20
- Forward P/E of 15

The lower forward multiple assumes earnings actually reach $4. If estimates fall to $2.50, the apparent cheapness disappears.

## What is a good P/E ratio?

There is no universally good number. A reasonable multiple depends on:

- Expected earnings growth
- Business quality and competitive durability
- Profit margins and capital requirements
- Debt and financial risk
- Cyclicality
- Interest rates and inflation
- Accounting quality

A stable company growing earnings at 4% may deserve a different multiple from a capital-light company growing at 20%. Compare the business with its history, appropriate peers, and realistic growth prospects.

## Why high-growth companies often have high P/E ratios

The market values future earnings, not only the latest year. If investors expect profits to grow rapidly, they may accept a higher current P/E.

Suppose two companies each trade at $60:

- Company A earns $3 per share and is expected to grow 5%.
- Company B earns $1 per share and is expected to grow 30%.

Their current P/E ratios are 20 and 60. Company B is more expensive based on present earnings, but its future results could narrow the difference. Whether it is attractive depends on how long growth lasts and what return investors require.

High expectations create risk. A company can report growth and still fall if results are weaker than the valuation already implied.

## When a low P/E is not cheap

A low multiple can be a warning:

- Earnings may be at a cyclical peak.
- The business may be shrinking.
- A major product or customer may be at risk.
- Debt or legal liabilities may threaten equity holders.
- Reported earnings may include a one-time gain.
- Heavy capital spending may make accounting profit overstate distributable cash.

Commodity producers and financial companies can look cheapest near peak earnings and most expensive near the bottom of a cycle. Normalize profits across a full cycle when possible.

## When P/E does not work

P/E is unavailable or unhelpful when earnings are negative. It can also mislead for:

- Early-stage companies with temporarily depressed profit
- Businesses with volatile or highly cyclical earnings
- Real-estate companies where depreciation changes the usefulness of net income
- Companies with materially different debt levels
- Firms reporting large non-recurring gains or losses

Alternatives include price-to-sales, price-to-free-cash-flow, enterprise-value multiples, asset value, and [discounted cash-flow analysis](/blog/how-to-value-a-stock).

## P/E and interest rates

Stocks compete with bonds and other assets for investor capital. When relatively safe interest rates rise, investors may demand higher returns from stocks. All else equal, paying a lower multiple helps provide that higher expected return.

This relationship is not mechanical. Rates can rise because economic growth is strong, while company-specific prospects can overwhelm broader valuation pressure.

## The PEG ratio

The price/earnings-to-growth ratio divides P/E by an expected earnings growth rate:

> PEG = P/E ratio ÷ expected annual EPS growth percentage

A P/E of 20 and growth estimate of 10% produces a PEG of 2.

The PEG ratio can help compare growth and valuation, but it compresses uncertainty into one figure. Growth forecasts may be wrong, growth quality differs, and the formula ignores debt, return on capital, and the duration of growth.

## Use P/E as a starting point

A practical review includes:

1. Confirm whether the ratio is trailing, forward, GAAP, or adjusted.
2. Reconcile EPS with the [financial statements](/blog/how-to-read-financial-statements).
3. Identify temporary gains, losses, and cyclical effects.
4. Compare growth, margins, leverage, and business quality with peers.
5. Test how the ratio changes under lower earnings assumptions.
6. Cross-check with cash flow and another valuation method.

## The bottom line

P/E shows how much the market currently pays for a dollar of earnings. It does not reveal whether those earnings are durable or whether forecasts will be achieved. Interpret the ratio in the context of growth, quality, risk, interest rates, and cash generation.
